# IRA CARE: validation interview guides

Written 26 November 2026. No interview has been conducted (`docs/evidence/ASSUMPTIONS_REGISTER.md`,
section 1). These guides are for the founders to run problem-discovery interviews before any
pilot or pricing decision. They are drafts: nothing here has been sent to anyone.

The aim is to learn how people deal **today** with the problems IRA CARE is designed for, not to
sell IRA CARE or collect compliments. A good interview is mostly about the interviewee's recent
past, in their own words.

## 1. Principles

- **Ask about past behaviour, not hypothetical futures.** "Tell me about the last time..." beats
  "Would you use...?". People are poor predictors of what they would buy.
- **No leading questions.** Do not name the problem you hope they have. Avoid "Don't you find...",
  "How useful would it be if...", "Wouldn't it be better...".
- **Do not pitch during the discovery part.** Show IRA CARE, if at all, only in the last ten
  minutes, and say plainly that it is a prototype with fictional data, not a product in use.
- **Listen for money, time and workarounds already spent.** A problem people already pay or work
  hard to solve is stronger evidence than one they agree with.
- **Silence is fine.** Let pauses run; do not fill them with suggestions.
- **Separate facts from opinions in the notes.** Record what happened, then what they think.
- **One interviewer, one note-taker where possible.** The note-taker writes verbatim quotations.

Questions to avoid, and better alternatives:

| Avoid (leading or hypothetical) | Ask instead |
| --- | --- |
| "Would a watch that checks in every 15 minutes help?" | "Walk me through an ordinary day for the people you support. When do you know they are all right?" |
| "Isn't it worrying when nobody answers the phone?" | "Tell me about the last time you could not reach your mother. What did you do?" |
| "How much would you pay for this?" | "What do you pay for at the moment to keep her safe? Who decided, and how?" |
| "Do falls cause you problems?" | "Tell me about the most recent fall you dealt with, from the moment you heard about it." |
| "Would your staff respond to alerts from an app?" | "What happens today when a telecare call comes through about one of your clients?" |

## 2. Ethics, consent and data protection

- **No clinical or care decisions.** Interviews gather opinions and experiences; they do not
  assess anyone's care needs or give advice. If a safeguarding concern arises, stop, follow the
  organisation's safeguarding route or the local authority's adult safeguarding line, and do not
  record the details in interview notes.
- **Capacity.** Interview only adults who can understand and agree to take part. Do not interview
  anyone whose capacity to consent is in doubt, and do not ask a relative to consent on their
  behalf for this kind of research.
- **Older adults.** Offer a family member or friend's presence if the person wants it, large-print
  information, and breaks. Keep sessions to 45 minutes or less.
- **Minimum data.** Do not ask for diagnoses, medication names, addresses or dates of birth. Record
  age band, living situation and role only.
- **No real personal data in this repository.** Raw notes, recordings and contact details stay in
  access-controlled storage outside the repository. Only anonymised, aggregated findings (for
  example "6 of 9 coordinators described...") may be added to the repository.
- **Retention.** Delete recordings once notes are checked (aim: within 30 days) and raw notes when
  the research round is written up, unless the person agreed otherwise.
- **No incentives** are offered at this stage (no budget is approved). If that changes, record it,
  because it can bias who takes part.
- **Lawful basis.** Before the first interview, record the UK GDPR lawful basis (likely
  legitimate interests for professionals; consent for older adults and family carers) and a
  short privacy notice. This needs checking by whoever acts as data protection lead; none is
  appointed (`docs/progress/GO_NO_GO.md`).

### Consent wording (read aloud and send in writing beforehand)

> Thank you for agreeing to talk to me. I am one of the founders of IRA CARE, a small company
> developing software to help older people who live alone get help when something goes wrong.
> It is at an early stage: it is a prototype and nobody is using it for real care.
>
> I would like to understand how you deal with these situations today. There are no right or
> wrong answers, and I am not trying to sell you anything.
>
> Taking part is voluntary. You can skip any question, stop at any time, and ask me to delete
> what you have said up to [date] without giving a reason.
>
> With your permission I will take notes [and make an audio recording, used only to check my
> notes and deleted within 30 days]. I will not record your name with your answers. I may quote
> your words in internal documents or in reports to people who fund or assess the company, but
> never in a way that identifies you or the people you care for.
>
> Please do not tell me anyone's name, address or medical details.
>
> Do you have any questions? Are you happy to go ahead? [Record "yes" with the date. If
> recording, ask separately: "Are you happy for me to record?"]

## 3. Recording the evidence

Use one record per interview. Keep the record outside the repository; copy only the anonymised
summary line into the findings table.

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Interview ID | e.g. `DOM-03` (segment code and number); never a name |
| Date, length, mode | In person, phone or video |
| Segment and screening answers | Role, size of organisation or living situation, age band |
| Consent recorded | Yes, with the wording version; recording yes or no |
| What happened (facts) | Recent specific incidents in their words: what, when, who, how long, what it cost |
| Current solution and spend | What they use or pay for now, who chose it, how long ago |
| Problems they raised unprompted | Only what they raised before you mentioned the area |
| Problems confirmed when prompted | Mark separately; weaker evidence |
| Verbatim quotations | Exact words, in quotation marks |
| Reaction to the prototype (if shown) | What they did and asked, not just what they said |
| Commitment offered | None; another meeting; an introduction; a pilot conversation; a letter. Commitments are the strongest evidence |
| Interviewer's interpretation | Clearly separate from the facts |

Evidence strength scale for the findings table:

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Opinion or agreement only ("that sounds useful") |
| 2 | Described a specific past incident matching the problem |
| 3 | Described current spend of money or staff time on the problem |
| 4 | Offered a commitment: introduction, second meeting with a decision-maker, pilot discussion |
| 5 | Signed or written commitment (letter of intent, pilot agreement) |

Do not report a level 1 finding as validation. Summaries should state counts ("4 of 7"), not
percentages, while samples are small.

## 4. Interview guides

Each guide: about 5 minutes of introduction and consent, 30 minutes of open questions, and an
optional 10 minutes showing the prototype. Probes in brackets are follow-ups, used only if the
answer is thin.

### 4.1 Care-home managers (segment code `CH`)

**Screening.** Registered manager or deputy of a CQC-registered care home in England for older
people; in post at least 6 months. Record bed count band (under 20, 20 to 49, 50 or more),
residential or nursing, and which care-record platform they use.

**Open questions.**

1. Tell me about the last night shift where something went wrong with a resident. What happened,
   and how did staff find out? (How long between the event and someone arriving?)
2. Walk me through how falls are noticed and handled here today. (What equipment? What happens
   after?)
3. What technology do you use to know where help is needed: call bells, sensor mats, acoustic or
   other monitoring? Who chose it, and what do you think of it now?
4. Tell me about the last time that technology let you down, or created extra work.
5. How do you decide how many staff are on at night? What happens when you are short?
6. How are families kept informed? Tell me about a recent difficult conversation with a family.
7. What have you bought or trialled in the last two years to make care safer or save staff time?
   What happened to it? (Who signed off the spend?)
8. If you could change one thing about how your team finds out that a resident needs help, what
   would it be? (Ask only at the end.)

### 4.2 Domiciliary care coordinators and registered managers (segment code `DOM`)

**Screening.** Coordinator, care manager or registered manager at a CQC-registered homecare
service in England; schedules or oversees visits to older people. Record service size band
(people supported: under 50, 50 to 199, 200 or more), mix of council-funded and self-funded
clients, and rostering or care-record platform.

**Open questions.**

1. Tell me about the last time a carer arrived and found a client who had fallen or been unwell
   since the previous visit. What happened next? (How long might they have been there?)
2. How do you find out today that something has gone wrong between visits? Who tells you, and how?
3. Tell me about the last call you received from a telecare service or a family member about a
   client out of hours. What did you do?
4. Which of your clients have a personal alarm or other technology? Who arranged it, and how do
   you hear about its alerts, if at all?
5. Walk me through a missed or late visit last month. What did it cost you, in time or money or
   relationships?
6. What does the on-call manager do on a typical night? What would add to that load?
7. What systems do you use for rotas, visit records and medication? What do they not do for you?
8. What have you paid for or trialled to keep clients safe between visits? What happened?

### 4.3 Family carers (segment code `FAM`)

**Screening.** An adult who regularly supports a parent, partner or relative aged 70 or over who
lives alone in England, and who is not paid to do so. Record the relative's age band, distance
between homes (same town, under 1 hour, over 1 hour), and whether paid carers visit. Do not record
the relative's health conditions.

**Open questions.**

1. Tell me about the last time you were worried about [your relative] and could not reach them.
   What did you do? How did it end?
2. How do you know, on an ordinary day, that they are all right? (Who else checks? How often?)
3. Tell me about a fall or emergency in the last year, if there was one. How did you find out,
   and how long after it happened?
4. What have you set up or bought to help: alarms, phones, cameras, sensors, smart speakers,
   neighbours? How did you choose, and what does it cost you each month?
5. Which of those does your relative actually use? Tell me about a time one was not used when it
   should have been.
6. How did your relative feel about it being set up? Who made the decision?
7. If paid carers visit, how do you hear about how the visits went?
8. What is the hardest part of keeping an eye on them from a distance?

### 4.4 Older adults living alone (segment code `OA`)

**Screening.** Aged 70 or over; lives alone in their own home in England; able to consent; happy
to talk for up to 45 minutes. Record age band and whether they have any alarm, smartwatch or
smartphone. Recruit through organisations (community groups, U3A, local Age UK branches), never
cold.

**Open questions.** Use plain words; one question at a time.

1. Tell me about a typical day for you. Who do you speak to, and when?
2. Has there been a time recently when you needed help and it was hard to get? What happened?
3. Have you ever had a fall, or been unable to get up? What did you do? How long was it before
   someone came? (Only if they raise falls; do not push.)
4. Do you have an alarm, button or watch for getting help? Tell me how you came to have it, and
   when you last used it. (If not, how would you get help today?)
5. How do you feel about your family or carers knowing how you are each day?
6. What do you use your phone (or other devices) for? What do you find fiddly?
7. Tell me about a gadget or service you gave up on. Why?
8. What matters most to you about staying in your own home?

If the prototype is shown: let them hold or see the watch screen, ask "What do you think this is
for?" before explaining, and note what they try to do.

### 4.5 Telecare monitoring centres (segment code `ARC`)

**Screening.** Manager, team leader or operator at a UK alarm receiving centre or telecare
service (council, housing or private). Record approximate number of connections monitored and
whether they hold a quality certification.

**Open questions.**

1. Walk me through the last fall call you handled, from the alert to the case being closed. (How
   long did the response take? Who went?)
2. What share of your calls are false or accidental activations? How do you handle them? (Ask for
   their own figures; do not suggest one.)
3. How has the digital switchover changed your work? Tell me about a problem you dealt with
   recently.
4. Which devices and protocols do you accept today? How does a new device supplier get connected?
   What did the last one have to do?
5. When you cannot reach a nominated responder, what happens?
6. What information do you wish you had at the moment a call comes in?
7. Who decides which new technology your service adopts, and what evidence did they ask for last
   time?

### 4.6 Local authority commissioners (segment code `LA`)

**Screening.** Commissioner, TEC lead or service manager for technology-enabled care, homecare or
reablement in an English local authority. Record the authority type (county, unitary,
metropolitan, London borough) and whether telecare is run in-house or contracted.

**Open questions.**

1. Tell me how technology-enabled care is organised and paid for here today. (Who provides it?
   When does the contract end?)
2. Walk me through how someone comes to get telecare after a request for support. How long does
   it take?
3. Tell me about the last new technology you trialled. Why, what evidence did you ask for, and
   what happened at the end?
4. What has the digital switchover meant for your service and budget?
5. How do you measure whether telecare is working for people? What data do you get from
   providers?
6. What would a new supplier need to have before you could consider them (frameworks, standards,
   assurance, data protection, clinical safety)?
7. Where do you see the biggest pressure in homecare and reablement this year?

## 5. Outreach email templates (drafts only; not sent)

Rules for every message: send from a named founder, one person at a time; say plainly that IRA
CARE is a prototype; ask for a short conversation, not a sale; no attachments with personal data;
remove the person from any list if they do not reply after one follow-up. Do not send to anyone
until the consent wording and privacy notice in section 2 are ready.

## 6. Sample size and what counts as a result

- Aim for 5 to 8 interviews per segment in the first round; stop a segment early if answers repeat.
- Write up each round as: what was heard (counts and evidence levels), what changed in our
  understanding, and which assumptions in `ASSUMPTIONS_FOR_PROJECTIONS.md` move from "Not known"
  or "Assumption" towards evidence.
- A finding enters the repository only as an anonymised summary with its counts and levels, and
  the date of the round.
